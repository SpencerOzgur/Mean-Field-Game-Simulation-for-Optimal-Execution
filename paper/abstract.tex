Market simulators are usually validated by how realistic their prices and order flow look. A trader uses them for something else: to decide how to execute. We ask whether simulators that all look plausible recommend the same execution schedule, and what it costs to trust one when another is true. One execution agent, one task and one cost accounting are run unchanged in six ABIDES configurations that differ only in their background population, and on a shorter task in a market driven by a TRADES diffusion model that we retrained on one public day of data. Costs are estimated against a same-seed twin with no intervention, across \nEpisodes{} ABIDES episodes. Four results follow. First, two of the six configurations, each one parameter away from the reference, are too unstable to evaluate anything (in one, the unperturbed price moves more than 1\% within 30 minutes in 70\% of runs), yet standard stylised facts do not flag them and they sit closest to a real reference day. Second, the four stable configurations agree: no policy pair is significantly ranked in opposite order in two of them, all four select an even schedule for an order of 20\% of volume, and the largest regret from choosing a schedule in one and deploying it in another is \maxRegretStable{} basis points. Third, a mean-field-game optimiser calibrated in each world does prescribe different schedules in different worlds, but the differences are calibration noise, and its front-loaded prescription costs two to three times as much as an even schedule where the difference is measurable. The retrained TRADES market, by contrast, gives the opposite advice to ABIDES (sell at once), because its quoted spread widens steadily once generation takes over from replay; this disagreement is real but is an artefact that a spread-versus-time check exposes. We did not detect a relation between the distance of two worlds' stylised facts and the regret of transferring between them, at a resolution too coarse to rule one out. ABIDES-MARL could not be included because no public implementation was found, and all regret is simulator-to-simulator: nothing here is validated against a real market.
