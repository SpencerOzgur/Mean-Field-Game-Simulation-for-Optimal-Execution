In plain words: what we have found so far

In simple words

Words used throughout this report:

bp (basis point)
0.01% of the order's value. On the $25 million example order, 1 bp = $2,500.
TWAP
The plain default: sell the same amount in every time step. Every other strategy is judged against it.
Simulator
A computer model of the stock market, used to try strategies before using them for real.
Regret
How much more the chosen strategy costs than the best possible strategy, in bp. Zero is perfect.
Seed / run
One simulated trading session. Each run has different random luck, so we average many runs.
True generator (oracle)
In our own synthetic markets we know the exact rules. Using those rules as the simulator is the best anyone could do.
Candidate
A simulator fitted to data from the market, the way a real researcher would build one.
Realism score
How much a simulator's market looks like the real one when nobody is trading against it.
Counterfactual / impact
How the price reacts when we trade: "what would have happened if we had sold?"
[a, b]
The 95% range for a result. If it includes zero, we cannot rule out "no effect".
Pre-registered
The test was written down and locked (with a digital fingerprint, SHA-256) before the results existed, so the goalposts could not be moved.

The running example. A trader must sell 500,000 shares. Assume $50 a share (roughly Intel's price in our data), so the order is worth $25 million, and 1 basis point (bp) of cost = $2,500. The trader can sell evenly over time (TWAP, the plain default), sell quickly, or follow a cleverer strategy that a market simulator recommends. A simulator is useful only if its recommendation saves money compared with just using TWAP.

The dollar figures below convert our basis-point results to an order of this value. They are illustrations: our tests used orders of 5–15% of the market's volume (2–30% in the 30-minute task), and costs per bp depend on the stock and the order's size relative to volume.

  • Simulators that look equally realistic give opposite advice. On real Nasdaq data (Intel, Cisco, Microsoft), the Hawkes simulator said "sell at once" and the ABIDES simulator said "spread the order out". They disagreed by 4.6–7.6 bp: on our order, $11,500–$19,000 depending on which one you believe. This held on all three stocks.
  • Looking realistic does not mean giving good advice. We built synthetic markets where we know the true cost of every strategy. No realism score (LOB-Bench, or LOB-Impact's free-running measures) told us which simulators would recommend good strategies. Simulators that copy the market's everyday statistics well can still be wrong about what happens when you sell 500,000 shares.
  • Getting your own order's price impact right helps a little, not reliably. Simulators that reproduce how prices react to a large order gave slightly better advice, but the effect was weak and not statistically significant once we corrected for testing several hypotheses.
  • Choosing the "best" strategy in a simulator picks its most flattering mistake. The strategy that looks cheapest is often the one whose cost the simulator underestimates most. On average its promised saving was overstated by about 10 bp: the simulator promises roughly $26,000 of savings on our order that do not exist.
  • Following fitted simulators lost money compared with plain TWAP. In markets with fundamental-value traders and liquidity providers who step back (agent-mix), following a typical fitted simulator cost about 5 bp more than TWAP (about $12,500 lost), and averaging all simulators about 6 bp (about $15,000). In a new family of markets that we held back until the end, the averaged simulators were 1.8 bp worse than TWAP (about $4,500). No method based on our simulators beat TWAP in any set of markets.
  • Running more simulations does not fix a wrong model; it makes it more confidently wrong. We took the true market model and switched off one ingredient, the traders who push the price back towards its fundamental value. With 10 runs it lost 7.9 bp against the best strategy; with 300 runs, 11.2 bp (about $28,000). It never found the true best strategy and overstated the saving from selling at once by about 18 bp (about $44,500). The full true model, by contrast, homed in on the right answer as runs increased in three of the four markets we tried.
  • Even a perfect simulator would save only a little over TWAP in most of our markets. Using the true model itself saved 0.4 bp over TWAP in the held-back markets (about $1,000), 1.7 bp in agent-mix markets (about $4,250) and 2.9 bp where the candidates had the right structure (about $7,250). So the prize for a good simulator is modest, while the penalty for a wrong one is several times larger.
  • Simulators do not follow changes in the market. When we turned one market ingredient up or down step by step, the fitted simulators picked the true best strategy at only 2–10% of the settings in agent-mix markets, and their predicted savings did not move with the true savings.
  • A longer job raises the stakes but not the simulators' usefulness. Over 30 minutes and 60 trading decisions, TWAP leaves 1–2.3 bp on the table ($2,500–$5,750) and the true model recovers most of it. No fitted simulator beat TWAP on average; on large orders the Hawkes simulators lost up to 25 bp in single markets (about $62,500).
  • Careful rules avoid the losses only by never trusting the simulator. Rules that depart from TWAP only when a simulator's predicted saving is large compared with its uncertainty avoided the losses, but in practice they almost always chose TWAP. That protects the trader; it does not make the simulator useful.
  • When strategies are almost equivalent, nothing matters much. In the queue-driven markets all strategies cost about the same (within 0.5 bp, about $1,250), so even poor simulators did no harm there.
  • Caveat: our synthetic markets are not Nasdaq. They match real data on random-walk behaviour and short-term return patterns, but prices in them keep moving after a trade for much longer than on Nasdaq and impact grows faster with order size. The conclusions describe markets with those features.

Bottom line for the trader with 500,000 shares. Today, the safest choice is TWAP, unless there is independent evidence that the simulator contains the mechanisms that drive the decision in this market (for example, whether prices are pulled back towards a fundamental value). A simulator's promised saving of, say, 8 bp ($20,000) is not evidence on its own: in our markets, promises of that size were mostly the simulator's own error. The next stage of the study (design addendum 10) tests whether checks that can be run on real market data can tell when a simulator's advice is safe to follow.

Statistics audit (simrel/reports/statistics_and_claims.md)

In simple words

We count markets, not simulation runs, as independent pieces of evidence, because thousands of runs of the same market still describe only one market. When we test several hypotheses we tighten the bar for each. After that adjustment none of the "this method helps" hypotheses is statistically firm. The finding that "no realism score helps" still holds.

  • Unit of replication: the reference market. Order sizes, candidate designs and seeds are never treated as independent; families (four) are reported separately, not resampled.
  • Intervals: markets, designs (one draw shared across markets) and seeds; realism scores held fixed; truth noise checked by cross-fitting.
  • Multiplicity: after a Holm adjustment no directional confirmatory hypothesis is significant (C1 one-sided p = 0.056; C2 p = 0.029, threshold 0.025). C3, a claim of absence, holds.
  • "Structural" error = candidates minus oracle at 100 seeds; it includes mechanism, parameter-estimation, model-selection and residual finite-sample error. Addendum 9 isolates the mechanism part; the well-specified markets isolate parameter error.
  • Independent tests are the drawn markets and the held-out family; the 100-seed reruns of the same markets are secondary.

1 Summary

In simple words

Simulators that look equally realistic can give opposite advice; looking realistic does not tell you which one gives good advice; and optimising a strategy inside a simulator that has the wrong rules leads to worse results than just using TWAP. The only hint of a useful signal, how well a simulator reproduces the price reaction to your own trade, is weak.

Three questions, three answers. (A) Can simulators reproduce real market behaviour? Partly: some match observational statistics, none passes the intervention tests. (B) Can they rank execution strategies? Often not, for two reasons: the decisions are flat or under-powered (even the true generator fails at our sample size) and, in the agent-mix markets, the mechanism is missing. (C) Can observable validation tell which simulator to trust? No: a substantive negative result. Reframed question: under what conditions does predictive error turn into poor decisions, and can validation detect those conditions before optimisation?

Main thesis. Observational market realism does not identify simulators that give reliable execution advice. The only property associated with better advice is how well a simulator reproduces the market's response to the specific trade, and that association is weak: it did not survive a pre-registered confirmatory test. Optimising over a simulator whose mechanism differs from the market's makes decisions worse than plain TWAP.

  1. Realistic simulators disagree. On three Nasdaq stocks, simulators with similar realism scores recommend opposite strategies: the original Hawkes model says "sell at once", the original ABIDES model says "spread the order out" (gap 4.6 to 7.6 bps, replicated under pre-registration on CSCO and MSFT).
  2. The disagreement has a measurable cause. The decision weighs two costs: selling at once (book depth) and selling gradually (impact of the trader's own order). No simulator gets both right, and which simulator gets impact right depends on the real-data benchmark used.
  3. Realism does not predict decision quality. In 26 synthetic markets where the true best strategy is known, no realism score from LOB-Bench or from LOB-Impact's free-run phase predicts low regret. This held in a confirmatory test on ten randomly drawn markets.
  4. Impact accuracy helps a little. Accuracy on the trader's own impact correlates +0.24 [0.01, 0.30] with good advice over the first ten markets, but the primary confirmatory test on ten new markets was not met (+0.15 [−0.03, 0.28]).
  5. Optimisation amplifies misspecification. Picking the cheapest predicted strategy selects the most flattering errors (over-promise of 10.4 bps against 1.6 bps for a typical strategy). Where the market lies outside the simulators' family, this is significantly worse than TWAP (+3.75 bps); inside it, it tends to beat TWAP (−1.96 bps, not significant). Conservative rules remove the damage but, tuned on misspecified markets, also never leave TWAP.

2 Questions and plan

In simple words

Goal 1 uses real stock-market data and asks whether realistic simulators at least agree with each other. Goal 2 builds synthetic markets where we know the right answer, so that advice can actually be graded. Real data cannot tell us who is right; synthetic markets can.

Goal 1
Does market realism translate into reliable execution decisions? Do simulators that look similarly realistic on real data recommend the same strategy?
Goal 2
Develop and validate a decision-aware evaluation: which kinds of realism predict good decisions, in markets where the right answer is known?

6 Measures

In simple words

Two families of scores. Realism: does the simulated market look like the real one when left alone (spreads, order sizes, price moves)? Counterfactual: when we trade in it, does the price react the way real prices react to a trade of that size? The second is closer to what an execution decision needs.

Realism flavour (observational)

In simple words

Scores computed from the market as it runs, with nobody trading against it.

  • LOB-Bench (ICML 2025, @276baf3): 21 default statistics compared by L1 (order book); conditional statistics; five return and volatility statistics by Wasserstein (price); LOB-Bench's impact score.
  • Trade response: mean price change ten seconds after the market's own aggressive orders.
  • LOB-Impact phase 1 (ICAIF 2026): trade-sign memory γ, Hurst exponent H, response ratio R(100)/R(1), free drift, from free runs.

Counterfactual flavour (interventional)

In simple words

Scores computed by sending our own orders into the simulator and measuring the price reaction.

  • LOB-Impact phases 2–3: a standard 100-child metaorder (median child size, 10% participation, buy and sell pooled); build-up exponent δ (signed binned estimator, day-clustered bootstrap), amplitude Y, peak impact, relaxation (end over peak after 100 cooling windows), visible over blind attribution (Hawkes only).
  • Metaorder impact error (ours, task-specific): the simulator's TWAP peak impact against the market's own, at the task's order size.
  • Validation profile (addendum 5): immediate-execution cost, TWAP impact, relaxation after TWAP, conditional price response, and, in synthetic markets only, cost-ranking accuracy.

Real-data checks (Goal 1)

In simple words

Two costs we can estimate from real data directly: what selling at once would have cost against the real order book, and how much gradual selling typically moves the price.

  • Cost of selling at once: walk the real held-out book (an upper bound; hidden liquidity lowers it by 7–17%).
  • TWAP impact: against the square-root law band (literature constants) and against impact measured on our own trades with synthetic metaorders.

9 Goal 2 design: markets with known answers

In simple words

Because real data cannot say which advice is right, we wrote our own markets, so the true cost of every strategy is known exactly. Each candidate simulator sees only six half-hour samples of a market, as a real researcher would, is fitted to them, and then recommends a strategy. We grade the recommendation by its regret.

Each reference market is a simulator whose rules we wrote, so every strategy's true expected cost can be computed (300 seeds per strategy and size). Candidate simulators see only six 30-minute observable episodes (event log and quotes), are fitted, scored for realism against twelve held-out reference windows, and asked for a recommendation (30 seeds). Regret is the true cost of the recommendation minus the true best.

Why TWAP wins: predicted against true gains

In simple words

The simulators think selling at once is about as cheap as TWAP; in truth it is about 8 bp worse ($20,000), yet it is their most frequent pick. This is the "optimizer's curse": if you choose whatever looks cheapest, you systematically choose the option whose cost was underestimated most, like picking the fund with the rosiest forecast and getting the most over-optimistic forecast.

  • Shared and idiosyncratic error: 29% of the error variance is shared by the simulators; 71% is specific to a simulator.
  • Ranking before selection is poor: median rank correlation of predicted and true costs over the 12 strategies is 0.22 (queue-reactive 0.42, agent-mix 0.02).
  • Selection amplifies error (the optimizer's curse): the chosen strategy's over-promise is +10.4 bps against +1.6 bps for a typical strategy. Promised and delivered gains of non-TWAP picks correlate −0.34; only 23% of them beat TWAP.
  • Oracle: removing the shared bias (using the truth) would give 1.62 bps against TWAP's 1.89.

13 Limitations

In simple words

What the study cannot show. The real data are limited (three large stocks, eight days, selling only). The markets with known answers are ones we wrote ourselves and differ from Nasdaq in some ways. Real data cannot say which simulator is right. With few markets, many ranges are wide. The 30-run problem in the second point below was later addressed by the 100-run reruns.

  • Three large US stocks, eight public sample days, sell orders, market orders only, ten-minute horizon.
  • Goal 2 decisions rest on 30 seeds per candidate, which the oracle shows is too few: sampling noise alone produces regret of the order of TWAP's in agent-mix and Hawkes-built markets. Queue-reactive decisions are flat.
  • Real data cannot say which simulator is right. Ground truth comes only from synthetic markets whose rules we wrote; the two synthetic families share mechanisms (mean reversion), which a prior can exploit.
  • The well-specified comparison rests on six markets and its favourable result for simulators is not significant.
  • LOB-Impact used 64 rollouts per arm over 8 days (paper: 1,024 over 20); ABIDES ran on a time clock, slow ABIDES runs were fitted into the trading day, and the Hawkes blind arm is only partly blind.
  • The real cost of selling at once is a band, not a point (hidden liquidity, other venues); real impact depends on the benchmark used.
  • Hawkes models miss sub-millisecond bursts; ABIDES-calibrated uses data-fitted quoting agents.
  • TRADES rests on one successfully trained seed and five training days.
